02 · The bet

Why I picked this fight.

Four short answers to the four questions every project should be able to answer before the first commit.

Signal

Construction site auditing is still clipboard, camera roll, and email threads.

Existing solutions are either cloud-based SaaS (requires connectivity on a construction site, which is laughable) or desktop software (requires a laptop, which is impractical on scaffolding). The gap is a professional-grade mobile tool that works entirely offline.

Filter

Vertical-specific, not horizontal.

Offline-first is the moat — most competitors assume connectivity. Encryption is the differentiator — site photos contain proprietary building details. Mobile-native is the delivery mechanism — inspectors are on-site, not at desks.

Why now

Post-pandemic infrastructure boom.

More construction sites, more inspections, more documentation requirements. Regulatory pressure for audit trails is increasing. Construction sites have terrible connectivity — basements, steel frames, remote locations. An offline-first tool isn't nice-to-have; it's the only thing that works on-site.

Why me

I've watched contractors try to document issues with personal phones.

Photos scattered across camera rolls, notes in half a dozen apps, no GPS coordinates, no severity tracking, no audit trail. Then hours back at the office trying to assemble a report from fragments.

03 · No-jargon explainer

Problem. Solution. Result.

01 · Problem

Construction professionals need to document issues, capture evidence, and generate reports — but the workflow is fragmented and insecure.

Photos live in personal camera rolls mixed with family pictures. Notes are scribbled in notebooks or scattered across apps. GPS coordinates are forgotten. Severity is subjective and inconsistent. Reports are assembled manually in Word or Excel hours after the site visit. Sensitive building details are stored on personal devices with no encryption.

02 · Solution

Build a completely offline-first mobile application purpose-built for site auditing.

Projects group all audit data. Issues capture title, description, category, severity, status, GPS location, assignee, due date, voice notes, and unlimited photos. Photos are captured via native camera then annotated with arrows, circles, rectangles, pen strokes, and text overlays — all with undo/redo history. Four pre-loaded inspection templates auto-generate starter issues. Eight branded PDF report templates generate professional deliverables. AES-256-GCM encrypts sensitive fields. Biometric authentication gates access. Everything stays on-device until the inspector decides to share.

01

Customer (Site Inspector)

I need to document issues on a construction site where there is no cell signal. I need to take photos, mark them up with arrows and circles, record voice notes when my hands are dirty, and generate a professional report with my company's logo before I leave the site. I need this to work on my phone without an internet connection. And I need to know that photos of proprietary building details aren't syncing to iCloud or Google Photos.

02

Business (Construction Company)

Liability is our biggest risk. An incomplete audit trail is a lawsuit waiting to happen. We need every issue documented with GPS coordinates, severity ratings, and timestamps. We need reports that look professional to clients and defensible in court. We need data to stay within our control — not on an inspector's personal camera roll or a SaaS vendor's cloud.

03

Operations (Project Manager)

I need to see which issues are open, which are resolved, and which are critical at a glance. I need to assign issues to contractors and track due dates. I need exports in formats my clients can open — PDF for executives, CSV for spreadsheets, JSON for our internal systems. I need the data to be portable when an inspector gets a new phone.

04

Legal & Privacy

Site photos often contain proprietary structural details, client information, and safety violations. Storing these on personal devices without encryption is a data breach. Storing them in cloud SaaS creates vendor lock-in and jurisdictional risk. The app must encrypt data at rest, exclude it from OS cloud backups, support biometric access control, and maintain an audit trail.

05

Growth

Network effect within a firm: one inspector adopting the tool creates pressure on colleagues to standardize. White-label potential for engineering firms wanting branded report templates. B2B licensing per firm or per inspector. Template marketplace for specialized inspection types. The offline-first architecture is the moat — competitors assume connectivity; this app works in a basement.

08 · Exhibit
▸ Decision · offline precision beats online convenience

When a structural engineer needs to circle a crack on a photo, not in Photoshop

The most distinctive feature of the app is the annotation canvas. A site inspector takes a photo of a structural crack, then needs to draw an arrow pointing to it, circle the affected area, and add a text label saying "reinforcement required — see report page 7." They do this on a construction site, often with gloves on, on a 3-year-old Android phone, with no internet. The canvas needs 5 tools, 7 colors, adjustable stroke width, and a full undo/redo history because accidentally clearing a complex annotation is a data-loss event. The interesting part isn't the drawing API — it's the product decision that an inspector needs Photoshop-grade markup on a phone, offline, with undo.

BEFORE: no annotation support 
function PhotoViewer({ photoUri }) {
  return (
    <Image source={{ uri: photoUri }}
      style={{ width: '100%', height: '100%' }}
    />
  );
}
// Inspector opens the photo in a separate app
// to annotate, loses the link between annotation
// and issue, forgets which annotation belongs
// to which photo.
AFTER: annotation canvas with undo/redo 
interface Stroke {
  tool: 'arrow' | 'circle' | 'rect'
       | 'pen' | 'text';
  points: { x: number; y: number }[];
  color: string;      // 7 preset colors
  width: number;      // 1-8 px
  text?: string;      // for text tool
}

function addStroke(stroke: Stroke) {
  const next = [...strokes, stroke];
  setStrokes(next);
  // Push to history, truncating forward
  const nextHistory = [
    ...history.slice(0, historyIndex + 1),
    next
  ];
  setHistory(nextHistory);
  setHistoryIndex(nextHistory.length - 1);
}

function undo() {
  if (historyIndex > 0) {
    setHistoryIndex(historyIndex - 1);
    setStrokes(history[historyIndex - 1]);
  } else if (historyIndex === 0) {
    setHistoryIndex(-1);
    setStrokes([]);
  }
}

// Strokes are serializable — survive restarts,
// export with photos, sync via backup.
▸ Why this matters

Including this exhibit because the interesting part is the design choice, not the drawing API. The decision to make annotations serializable (so they survive app restarts and export with photos) and the undo/redo history stack (so 20 strokes of careful markup aren't lost to a single mis-tap) are product decisions that happen to be implemented in TypeScript. The technology is trivial; the design is the story.

10 · Decisions

Five questions worth answering slowly.

Short answer first — long answer if you need it.

Q01Why completely offline-first?+

Construction sites have no cell signal in basements, steel frames, and remote locations. An app that requires connectivity fails where inspectors actually work.

The offline-first architecture isn't a fallback mode — it's the only mode. All data is stored locally in SQLite. All photos are on the file system. All exports are generated client-side. The app never pings a server for core functionality. This makes it faster (no network latency), more reliable (no connection drops), more private (no data leaves the device), and cheaper (no SaaS subscription). The tradeoff is no real-time collaboration — but structural engineers don't need Slack, they need a tool that works in a basement.

Q02Why SQLite instead of a cloud database?+

SQLite is queryable, transactional, and requires zero network. It's the right database for a device that may not have connectivity for days.

Cloud databases require connectivity, have latency, and create vendor lock-in. SQLite is embedded, ACID-compliant, and well-supported on mobile via expo-sqlite. WAL mode improves concurrency. Foreign keys enforce referential integrity. Parameterized queries prevent SQL injection. The database file can be backed up, encrypted, and migrated as a single unit. For a tool that must work without a network, an embedded database isn't a compromise — it's the correct architecture.

Q03Why field-level encryption instead of full-disk?+

Full-disk encryption protects against device theft but not against unlocked-device access. Field-level encryption adds a second gate: even if the phone is unlocked, sensitive data remains encrypted until biometric auth succeeds.

Modern phones have full-disk encryption. This protects data at rest if the device is stolen and powered off. But it does nothing if the device is unlocked and an unauthorized user picks it up. Field-level AES-256-GCM encryption with device-bound keys in expo-secure-store means that even on an unlocked phone, template content and backup archives are ciphertext without biometric authentication. The encryption keys never leave the secure enclave. This is defense in depth: disk encryption for theft, field encryption for unauthorized access.

Q04Why React Native instead of Flutter?+

Existing skill graph, component library, and Expo SDK 52 native modules favored React Native without ejecting.

Flutter is a valid alternative with excellent performance. React Native was chosen because: (1) existing codebase and team expertise were React/TypeScript-based, (2) Expo SDK 52 provides first-class native modules for every requirement without ejecting, (3) HeroUI Native provided accessible, well-designed primitives, (4) the React Native ecosystem has better support for Skia-based 2D drawing needed for the annotation canvas.

Q05Why dark-first design?+

Construction sites are bright and dusty. Inspectors view screens outdoors in direct sunlight. A dark UI with high-contrast orange accents is more legible in bright conditions.

The design philosophy "Precision Craft" rejects generic white-background aesthetics. Construction sites are visually noisy — concrete, steel, dust, bright sunlight. A dark interface with burnt orange accents provides high contrast that's readable in direct sunlight. Dark mode also reduces battery drain on OLED screens and causes less eye strain during all-day site visits. Light mode is available for indoor report review, but dark mode is the default because it's the mode inspectors need most often.

12 · Reflection

What surprised me. What I'd do differently. What's next.

Surprised by

How much the annotation canvas mattered.

It was originally a nice-to-have feature, but during early testing, every inspector gravitated to it immediately. The ability to draw an arrow to a crack and circle a problem area turned out to be the feature that made the app feel professional instead of just functional. The undo/redo history stack was added after watching an inspector accidentally clear 12 strokes of careful markup and sigh with frustration. That single interaction changed the priority from P2 to P0.

Would do differently

Build the export pipeline earlier.

PDF generation with expo-print has platform-specific quirks (page breaks, image scaling, font embedding) that surfaced late in the build. Starting the export service in phase 3 instead of phase 4 would have caught these issues before the UI was fully polished around incorrect assumptions. The template system also needed more user input — the 4 pre-loaded templates are generic, and every firm wanted custom templates from day one.

What's next

Template marketplace, cloud sync opt-in, iPad optimization.

Custom template marketplace for specialized inspections (electrical, plumbing, structural, safety, environmental). Cloud sync as an opt-in feature for firms that want central data repositories while keeping offline-first as default. iPad optimization for larger annotation canvas. Integration with Bluebeam or AutoCAD for structural drawings. Team collaboration via peer-to-peer sync on local WiFi.
